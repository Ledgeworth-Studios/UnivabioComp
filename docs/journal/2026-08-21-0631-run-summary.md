### What it actually does now

Open it, type a condition, pick somewhere near you. You get the recruiting trials
back, each with the nearest site and how far away it is, what would stop you and
why, quoting the registry's own words, and what to ask the study team. Correct
anything we got wrong by clicking a chip and the results update. Press one button
for a printable page to take to your appointment.

Three moments worth putting in the demo video, all real, all verified live:

1. **Set the age chip to 41, then 12, then clear it.** The same six trials
   reorder and re-explain themselves each time. At 41 the paediatric MS trial says
   *"This trial enrols ages 10 Years to 17 Years; you told us you are 41 years
   old"* and sinks to the bottom. At 12 the adult trials do. Cleared, they all say
   *"not settled"* — never *"you don't qualify"*.
2. **A study with ninety-one sites shows the one 0.6 miles from downtown
   Portland.** The registry lists Birmingham, Alabama first. The obvious
   implementation sends a Portland user to Alabama.
3. **A trial that looks like an ordinary adult diabetes study** — right ages,
   right sexes, healthy volunteers welcome — carries a caution saying its
   participants appear to be *health centres*, with the three pieces of evidence
   printed underneath. One of forty live diabetes trials was flagged, and it was
   the right one.

### Three decisions the human should read and may overrule

They are in `docs/decisions/`, and each one is a place a reasonable person could
choose differently:

- **0002** — trials you conflict with are ranked down and **never hidden**. Every
  other trial finder filters them out. The whole product is called *Why Not This
  Trial*; a person who sees four results cannot tell whether a fifth was removed
  for a reason they could have argued with.
- **0003** — a trial that looks like it enrols organisations is **labelled, not
  hidden and not demoted**, and needs two independent signals before we say
  anything. A false positive that adds a caution costs ten seconds; one that
  buries the trial costs somebody a study they could have joined.
- **0004** — **an agent may propose a label for the eval set; it may not confirm
  one.** And `MET` on an exclusion criterion means the sentence describes you,
  which is what rules you out.

## Why I did it this way

**I split three tasks rather than half-finishing them.** W2-2 said "search +
judge", W3-3 said "questions from UNKNOWNs", and both had a half that needs the
model. Each was cut at the blocker: the deterministic half done properly, the
judge-dependent half filed as `W2-2b` / `W3-3b` and marked `BLOCKED`. Nothing was
descoped, and no task was closed on work that had not been done.

**I marked six tasks `BLOCKED` that were sitting as `READY`.** W3-1 and all of
Week 4 after W4-1 need the judge. A `READY` task that cannot be finished costs a
scheduled run its entire turn — which had already happened on this project before
W1-6 and W2-1 were marked. Each carries the reason and what would unblock it.

**I stopped a task once and put it back.** W5-1's fourth criterion is a race
condition, and a race cannot be verified by looking at a page. `web/` had no test
runner, so I set W5-1 back to `READY`, wrote D-5 as its prerequisite, did D-5,
came back and finished it. That cost two commits and kept one task per commit
intact.

**I verified against the live registry, not just fixtures.** Every user-facing
task in this run was checked in a real browser against real ClinicalTrials.gov
data, including one verification done by **stopping the backend** to see the
error, and one done by **wrapping `fetch` in a 1.5 second delay** because the
response cache kept answering faster than the in-flight state could be sampled.
If you cannot observe a state reliably, slow the thing down rather than sampling
harder.
