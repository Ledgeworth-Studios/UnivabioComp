# D-2 — "27 Weeks" might be a pregnancy, and the code was reading it as an age

**Status:** DONE
**Files touched:** `whynot/hardfilter.py`, `tests/test_hardfilter.py`,
`docs/decisions/0006-gestational-age-bounds.md` (new), `BACKLOG.md`

## What I did

Fixed the worst latent bug in this project, left behind deliberately by W1-3 with
a note saying it must not be forgotten.

The registry gives eligibility ages as strings and `hardfilter.py` converts them
all to years and compares. For one kind of trial that is simply wrong.
`NCT01066728` states its bounds as **27 Weeks to 32 Weeks**, and its own criteria
text says what they are:

> *"Infants between 27 and 32 weeks **gestational age** hospitalized in the
> neonatal intensive or intermediate care units."*

Those are weeks **of pregnancy at birth**, not weeks of life. Converted as ages
since birth they become about 0.52 to 0.62 years. A two-week-old baby is 0.04
years, falls outside, and the code said **`NOT_MET`**.

In plain terms: it told the parents of a premature baby that their child does not
qualify for a trial their child may well qualify for. That is precisely the error
`docs/PLAN.md` says to weight above every other.

The registry has no field saying which kind of age a bound is. Only the prose
does, and not always.

## The rule, and why it is not "treat weeks as ambiguous"

The naive fix is to distrust every bound written in weeks. It would break trials
that are perfectly clear: `NCT06737159` enrols children from **5 Weeks** to 24
Months with bronchiolitis. Five weeks is not a length of pregnancy, and that trial
can give a straight answer.

So a bound counts as possibly gestational when it is **in weeks and between 20 and
45** — roughly the edge of viability to a little past term. Both cases are real
records already in the test fixtures, and both are covered.

Then the verdict rule, which I think is the more valuable half of this task
because it generalises:

> **A `NOT_MET` requires that no reasonable reading of the registry's wording
> admits the person.**

For `NCT01066728` that produces three different answers:

| Person | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Two weeks old | `UNKNOWN` — excluded on one reading, possibly fine on the other |
| Seven months old | `UNKNOWN` — *inside* the bounds read as age since birth, but under the gestational reading the trial wants a baby born at 27–32 weeks and the profile does not say |
| 41 years old | `NOT_MET` — no reading admits them; under the gestational reading this enrols newborns and they are not one |

The middle row is the one I nearly got wrong. Being inside the bounds on one
reading is not the same as knowing the answer, and `MET` there would be a claim
the data cannot support.

The last row matters too: **ambiguity is not an excuse to say nothing.** Returning
`UNKNOWN` for every adult who searches would be noise, and noise is how a useful
`UNKNOWN` gets ignored.

The `UNKNOWN` reason says: *"For studies of newborns that usually means weeks of
pregnancy at birth rather than age since birth, and the registry record does not
say which. Ask the study team which one they mean."* Which is exactly the question
a parent should ask.

## Why I did it this way

**One signal is enough here, and that is the opposite of `docs/decisions/0003`.**
W3-4 demands *two* independent signals before labelling a trial as enrolling
organisations. Here a single signal — a weeks bound in the gestational range — is
enough to withhold a `NOT_MET`, even though the criteria text saying "gestational"
is available as corroboration.

The rules point opposite ways because the errors do. There, a false positive makes
a claim about a trial and could bury it, so corroboration protects the reader.
Here the trigger *removes* a claim, so acting on one signal is the cautious
direction — and demanding corroboration would let the dangerous case through every
time a record happened not to use the word. Same project, opposite rule, and the
reason is which way the harm runs. That is written up in
`docs/decisions/0006`.

**Two years for the newborn window, deliberately generous.** It decides only
whether we are willing to say `NOT_MET`, so erring large errs towards `UNKNOWN`.

## What the next run should know

1. **All 179 previous tests passed unchanged after the fix**, which tells you
   nothing had ever asserted the dangerous behaviour — the bug was live and
   untested, not a deliberate choice somebody pinned. There are 17 new tests;
   the suite is now 196.
2. **The general principle in `docs/decisions/0006` is worth more than the
   specific fix.** Any future reading of registry data where two interpretations
   disagree should follow it: if one reading rules the person out and another does
   not, the answer is `UNKNOWN`. The weeks case is only its first instance.
