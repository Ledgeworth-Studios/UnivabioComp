## What I did

Week 4 is about measuring whether the judge is any good. To measure it you need
an answer key: real eligibility criteria, paired with a described person, and the
verdict a careful reader would give. This task builds that key — **31 pairs**,
drawn from four real recruiting trials and three hand-written people.

The set is generated by a script (`tools/build_eval_set.py`) rather than typed,
and the loader (`whynot/evalset.py`) enforces two rules that matter more than the
code does.

### Rule 1: the criteria must be real, and it is checked

`load_eval_set` refuses any pair whose criterion text does not appear **verbatim**
in one of the recorded registry responses. There is a test that tries to sneak in
`"Must have a positive attitude and a can-do spirit"` and asserts it is rejected.

The reason is that a plausible-sounding invented criterion would make the eval
measure the wrong thing while looking completely healthy. Registry prose is
messier than anything a person writing test data would produce — W1-4 found
markdown-escaped numbering, roman-numeral sub-items and headers written as
bullets — and an eval made of tidy sentences would flatter the judge.

### Rule 2: an agent may propose a label. It may not confirm one.

This is the important one, and it is written up as `docs/decisions/0004`.

The eval set is the answer key. **If the same kind of system writes both the
answers and the key, the resulting number measures agreement rather than
correctness** — and that number is going in front of competition judges attached
to a claim about a medical tool.

So every pair carries the proposed verdict, the reasoning in a sentence, whether
settling it takes judgement, and who confirmed it. Of the 31 pairs, **26 need no
review and 5 do**, and the 5 are not passed to the scoring code at all until
somebody puts their name against them. Not a warning that gets ignored — they are
excluded from the count, and the count of exclusions is reported next to the
score.

The 26 are labels anybody can check by reading. The profile records no MRI
findings, so a criterion about gadolinium-enhancing lesions is `UNKNOWN`. The
profile says 41 and the criterion says "Age ≥ 22", so it is `MET`. No medical
knowledge is involved, and these are the majority of what matters — the dangerous
error is `NOT_MET` where the truth is `UNKNOWN`, and it lives precisely in the
"profile is silent" category.

The 5 flagged ones are the genuinely interpretive questions, and they are good
ones for the human to think about:

- Does somebody's own statement that they have MS satisfy *"Confirmed diagnosis
  of MS by a physician, who is a neurologist"*? (Three pairs ask a version of
  this. A reviewer should answer it once and consistently.)
- Does *"relapsing-remitting MS"* mean a person is not excluded by *"Diagnosis of
  progressive forms of MS"*?

### The thing this task forced into the open

Writing the set surfaced a question W1-5 had left implicit, and it would have
been expensive to find later: **what does `MET` mean on an exclusion criterion?**

"Diagnosis of progressive forms of MS" is an exclusion. If a person has
progressive MS, is that criterion `MET`? `docs/decisions/0004` settles it: **`MET`
means the criterion, as written, describes the person** — so yes, and being `MET`
there is exactly what rules them out. `MET` never means "good news".

The alternative — `MET` meaning "satisfied in the direction of enrolling" — was
rejected because it makes the judge work out the polarity of each sentence before
answering, in a blob of prose whose headers are sometimes missing entirely.

**W1-5's prompt must state this convention.** If the judge assumes the other one,
every exclusion criterion scores backwards and the overall number still looks
plausible. That is now written down in two places and flagged on the eval pair
most likely to expose it.

## What the next run should know

1. **There are two human actions blocking Week 4 now, not one.** The API key, and
   a review of the 5 flagged labels. Neither is something an agent should do. The
   review is maybe fifteen minutes of reading, and the questions are listed above.
2. **The set is 2 `MET`, 2 `NOT_MET`, 27 `UNKNOWN`, and that lopsidedness is
   honest but limiting.** It reflects what actually happens: our profile has four
   fields — age, sex, healthy-volunteer, and a list of condition names — so almost
   any real criterion is unanswerable from it. That makes the set a good
   instrument for the error we care most about and a weak one for everything else.
   Which leads to:
3. **`PatientProfile` is too thin, and that is now `D-6`.** A profile that cannot
   represent "diagnosed in 2019", "currently on ocrelizumab", "EDSS 3.5" can never
   produce a `MET` on the criteria that actually decide trials. When W2-1
   (extraction) is built it will want somewhere to put those, and the eval set
   cannot get much richer until it has one.
4. **`test_nobody_has_reviewed_the_shipped_set_yet` is designed to fail.** The day
   a human reviews the flagged pairs, that test goes red on purpose — it is a
   reminder to update the journal and to state, wherever the eval number appears,
   how many pairs were scored and how many were held back.
5. **W4-2, W4-3 and W4-4 all need the model**, so after this the file order runs
   out of unblocked Week 4 work. A run picking up from here should check whether
   they want marking `BLOCKED`, the way W1-6 and W2-1 were, so a future run does
   not take one and lose its turn.
